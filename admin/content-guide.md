# Ink Journey — Content Generation Guide

This document is the reference for writing and generating stories, landmarks, and journeys for Ink Journey. Use it directly as a prompt supplement when generating content with an AI, or as a guide when writing manually.

---

## What Ink Journey Is Trying to Do

Ink Journey turns a walk into a discovery. The user is moving through a real place, and the content should make that place feel more alive, more layered, and more worth paying attention to. The emotion we're after is the feeling of finding something — a small secret, a human trace, a reason the world is more interesting than it looked.

**Behaviours we need to encourage:**
- Keep walking (don't stop and read, then go home)
- Look around after reading (the content points at the world, not at itself)
- Want to come back (curiosity outlasts the current session)
- Post their own stories (feel like their observation belongs here too)

---

## The Core Formula

Every story, landmark, and journey chapter should do these four things:

```
Hook → Truth → Human Moment → Mystery
```

| Element | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | Earns the next sentence | "This road is named after something that no longer runs beside it." |
| **Truth** | One surprising fact they didn't know | "Before the railway arrived, everything for six miles of homes came through here by barge." |
| **Human Moment** | Something a person did, felt, or experienced here | "The last barge keeper lived in that cottage well into the 1970s." |
| **Mystery** | A reason to keep going | "There's still one thing on this stretch that hasn't changed since then." |

Not every standalone story needs all four — a short two-sentence story can just have hook + truth, or truth + human moment. Journeys should hit all four per chapter.

---

## Tone

**The voice is:** human, observant, grounded in truth, slightly warm, occasionally wry. It sounds like a very well-read local — someone who knows things but doesn't perform knowing things.

**It is not:** an encyclopedia, a tour guide script, a travel blog, forced poetry, or a Wikipedia summary.

The content should feel *discovered*, not posted.

### Quick tests
- Would a person say this while walking with a friend? ✓
- Does it tell you something you didn't know? ✓
- Does it make you look at the place differently? ✓
- Could it have been written about anywhere? ✗
- Does it feel like a Wikipedia article? ✗
- Is it trying too hard to be beautiful? ✗

---

## Story Format Rules

- **Length:** 1–4 sentences for stories. Landmarks can go slightly longer (2–5 sentences + practical info).
- **Location first:** every story must only make sense where it's placed. Remove the location and it should fall apart.
- **Tense:** past tense for seeded/curated content (things happened before the app went live). Present tense only where it genuinely fits the observation.
- **Person:** first or third both valid. Seeded stories often read better in third. User-style stories feel more natural in first.
- **Endings:** don't wrap up — leave a small gap. The reader should feel slightly unfinished.

---

## What to Avoid

### Generic filler
> Nice park. / Lovely area. / Great place to visit.

### Dry encyclopedia tone
> Built in 1909. Renovated in 1986. Grade II listed since 2003.

### Forced poetry
> The town breathes through ancient veins. / History whispers on every cobblestone.

### No-payoff endings
> That is the history of this road. / And that is why this place matters.

### Anything that could be anywhere
> A busy street with lots of history. / People have lived here for centuries.

### Over-explaining
> This is interesting because... / What this means is... / In other words...

## Story Archetypes

These patterns generate reliably good stories across different locations.

### 1. The Named Thing (best for streets, parks, buildings)
Something nearby is named after something that no longer exists — a river that was buried, an industry that closed, a building that burned down.
> "The road is called Navigation Road. The navigation it was named after is still there — barely."

### 2. The Last / First / Only
A superlative that's specific to this place: the last remaining, the first in the region, the only one left of its kind.
> "This is the last stretch of the original cobbled lane that ran from the market to the river. Everything else was resurfaced in the 1960s."

### 3. The Ordinary Extraordinary
A mundane place that turns out to have an unexpectedly significant history.
> "This car park used to be the town's principal livestock market. For a century, farmers brought cattle through what is now the entrance to Sainsbury's."

### 4. The Trace
Something small and overlooked that's actually a remnant — a wall, a line in the pavement, an old sign.
> "The faint line in the brickwork isn't a repair. It's the original boundary of a building that stood here for two hundred years."

### 5. The Human Habit
Not history — an observation about what people do here, have always done here, or used to do here that reveals something about the place.
> "Every morning at the same time, the same man reads his paper at that bench. He's been doing it for eleven years. He says the tram noise helps him concentrate."

### 6. The Before / After
The place was completely different, and imagining it changes how you see it now.
> "Forty years ago, this was a derelict canal wharf. Today it's one of the most photographed food markets in the north. What happened in between is worth knowing."

### 7. The Overheard / Witnessed
A small moment that happened here — real or representative — that grounds the place in human experience.
> "Someone left a note on this bench last winter. It said: 'I used to come here with my dad. He would have liked what it's become.'"

### 8. The Unsolved / Unexplained
Something about the place that doesn't quite add up, has an uncertain history, or has multiple competing explanations.
> "Nobody is quite certain why this street ends where it does. The most convincing theory involves a property dispute that was never officially resolved."

## Journey Chapter Formula (extended)

For curated journeys, each chapter gets slightly more space and should hit all four formula elements.

### Chapter structure
1. **Opening hook** — one sentence that creates immediate curiosity
2. **The truth** — one surprising fact in one or two sentences
3. **Human detail** — who was here, what they did, how it felt
4. **Pull-forward** — one line that makes the next chapter feel necessary

### Emotional pacing across a journey
Don't make every chapter the same energy. A good journey alternates:
```
Busy / arrival → Quiet / neighbourhood → Discovery / connection → Green relief → Reflection → Reward / return
```

Vary: mystery ↔ warmth ↔ humour ↔ melancholy ↔ wonder

### Micro-cliffhangers (end-of-chapter lines)
These are reusable patterns for the pull-forward line:
- "One trace of that history still survives — it's ahead."
- "Most people who walk this route never notice what it was built for."
- "The strangest part of this story is still a few minutes away."
- "There's a reason this road bends exactly where it does."
- "What happened next changed this part of town permanently."

## Annotated Examples

### ✗ Weak
> Grey Road connects homes to town.

*Why it fails: tells us nothing. Could be anywhere. No hook, no truth, no human detail, no mystery.*

### ✓ Strong
> Grey Road looks forgettable, but roads like this quietly built modern Altrincham. Every morning, hundreds of small routines still flow through it. There's a reason the path ahead feels older than the houses beside it.

*Why it works: starts with a light subversion (it looks forgettable, but), gives a truth (roads like this built the town), lands on a mystery (why does it feel older?).*

---

### ✗ Weak
> This park opened in 1909.

### ✓ Strong
> This park opened in 1909 — but the oldest thing here was already waiting long before the council arrived.

*Why it works: same fact, but now the fact creates a question instead of closing one.*

---

### ✗ Weak
> The canal transported coal.

### ✓ Strong
> Before the railway arrived, coal for every home and workshop for six miles came through here by barge. The canal didn't just move goods — it decided where towns grew.

*Why it works: puts coal in context of people's lives, adds a consequence (where towns grew), implies something worth knowing.*

## Place Knowledge: Altrincham

Use these facts as raw material. Transform them using the formula — don't use them as-is.

### Character of the place
Altrincham is an old market town absorbed into Greater Manchester's commuter belt, but it hasn't fully lost its own identity. It has the texture of a place that knows what it used to be. The 2014 market revival became nationally significant — Altrincham Market was cited by the government and food press as a model for high street regeneration, used as a case study across the UK. The town has money now, but the bones underneath are working-class and agricultural.

### Key facts and hooks

**The Market**
- Altrincham Market has been operating since 1290 — one of the oldest markets in England by royal charter
- The modern market revival (2014) was so successful it was referenced in national planning guidance and studied by other councils
- The original market site is still the market site — centuries of continuity on one patch of ground

**Transport**
- Navigation Road is named after the Bridgewater Canal's navigation — the canal passes close by
- The Bridgewater Canal (opened 1761) was the first true modern canal in Britain, predating the canal mania by decades
- Altrincham had one of Britain's earliest electric tramways — the Altrincham & Stretford Electric Tramway (1882) was among the first electric tram systems in the world
- The Metrolink line follows almost exactly the route of the old railway/tram line — the infrastructure is a palimpsest

**Industry**
- The Linotype factory in Broadheath (Altrincham) was one of the most significant printing technology manufacturing sites in Britain. Linotype machines — which revolutionised newspaper and book printing — were made here and exported worldwide. The factory has since closed.
- Broadheath industrial estate was one of the largest planned industrial areas in the north of England in the early 20th century

**The Canal**
- The Bridgewater Canal was financed by the Duke of Bridgewater specifically to undercut the price of coal in Manchester — it halved coal prices in the city within a year of opening
- The canal network around Sale/Timperley/Altrincham still carries leisure boats but was once heavy commercial traffic
- Dunham Massey (National Trust deer park, nearby) was historically connected to the canal — the estate used it for transport

**Neighbourhoods**
- Hale and Bowdon (adjacent to Altrincham) are Victorian/Edwardian wealthy commuter villages — the railway made them. Without the railway, they would likely have remained small agricultural settlements.
- Old Market Place is the medieval core — the rest grew outward from here
- The street pattern in the old town predates modern planning; roads bend and terminate oddly because they follow medieval field and property boundaries

**Local character**
- The town has a strong independent food/drink scene centred on the market — nationally recognised
- Altrincham FC (non-league) has one of the most passionate local football followings in non-league football; the club has a long identity separate from the Manchester clubs
- The area between Altrincham and Sale has significant green space — the Mersey Valley corridor

### Journey theme ideas for Altrincham

| Theme | Hook | Emotional arc |
|---|---|---|
| **Seven Centuries of Market** | The same patch of ground has been a market since 1290 | wonder → connection → pride |
| **The Canal That Changed Manchester** | One waterway halved coal prices and built the industrial north | history → scale → reflection |
| **Broadheath: When Altrincham Made the World's Newspapers** | Linotype machines built here were in every newspaper office on earth | discovery → scale → melancholy (it's gone) |
| **The First Electric Town** | Altrincham ran electric trams before almost anywhere else in Britain | surprise → detail → continuity |
| **The Revival** | How a dying market town became a national case study | recent history → human effort → optimism |
| **Old Town, New Town** | Walk from the medieval core outward, each street marking a different century of expansion | observation → layering → wonder |

### General London hooks (applicable across areas)
- Almost every tube station name hides a buried river, a demolished building, or a lost village
- The boundary between ancient parishes is often still visible in street patterns — a sudden kink in a road marks an old edge
- London was a collection of separate towns until the Victorian railway and tram networks fused them — most areas still have a "village centre" structure underneath the urban fabric
- The Great Fire of 1666 only burned the City — everything north, south, and east was untouched medieval street pattern until Victorian development
- Many London streets are named after landowners, not places — the name tells you who owned the fields

### Themes that work across London
- Hidden rivers (Effra, Fleet, Walbrook, Tyburn — most still flow underground)
- The buried village (every area was once separate)
- The bomb damage map (WWII damage is still readable in the architecture — abrupt style shifts, mid-century infill)
- The market that moved / the industry that left
- The migration layer (who came here and why, what they built)

## AI Generation Prompt Template

Use this as a starting point when generating new content:

### For a standalone story
```
Write a 2–3 sentence story for Ink Journey, a location-based storytelling app.
Location: [specific place name and what it is]
Facts available: [paste 1–3 facts about the location]

The story should:
- Open with a hook that creates immediate curiosity
- Include one surprising truth or human detail
- End without fully resolving — leave a small gap
- Sound like a well-read local, not a tour guide
- Be specific to this location (remove the location and the story falls apart)

Avoid: generic language, encyclopedia tone, forced poetry, wrap-up endings.
```

### For a journey chapter
```
Write a journey chapter for Ink Journey. This is stop [N] of [total] in a walking journey called "[journey title]".

Location: [specific place]
Chapter role in journey: [opening / middle / climax / ending]
Emotional note for this chapter: [mystery / warmth / discovery / melancholy / wonder]
Facts available: [paste 2–4 facts]

Structure: Hook → Truth → Human Moment → Mystery
Length: 3–5 sentences
End with a pull-forward line that makes the next chapter feel necessary.
Tone: human, grounded, slightly warm, specific. Not a tour guide.
```

### For a full journey
```
Write a [N]-stop walking journey for Ink Journey set in [location].

Each chapter must contain:
1. One surprising truth about the specific location
2. One human detail (what people did, felt, or experienced here)
3. One pull-forward line (a reason to walk to the next stop)

Tone: human, factual, emotionally engaging. Like a well-read local, not a tour guide.

Emotional arc: start with arrival energy, vary between discovery/warmth/mystery, end with reflection or reward.

Stops: [list GPS locations or street names in order]
Theme: [e.g. canal heritage / market history / Victorian expansion]
```

## User-Generated Content Prompts

These are prompts we can surface in the app to help users write better stories:

- "What surprised you here?"
- "What do people do here that they probably don't think about?"
- "What was here before this?"
- "What would make someone walk to where you're going next?"
- "What would you tell a friend who was about to walk past this?"
